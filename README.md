Food webs provide critical insight into species interactions and ecosystem stability, yet their structural rules have rarely been tested  across environmental differences. This study addresses whether established scaling laws hold across both tropical and temperate streams. 
In food web theory, the link-species scaling law predicts that the number of trophic links (L) increases linearly with the number of species (S). Additionally, connectance (C), defined as the proportion of realized trophic links relative to all possible links (C = L/S²), is expected to decline with increasing species richness. Early studies have primarily been conducted in temperate streams, however, tropical streams possess many characteristics that differ, preventing us from making generalizations about ecosystem patterns. 
We constructed two tropical stream food webs for Río Piedras and Quebrada Gatos in Puerto Rico using data collected from the gut contents of dominant taxa in each stream. These streams were included in a dataset compiled from available stream food webs which was used to compare food web structure (S, L, C)  in temperate and tropical regions with a mixed effects model. This method allows us to assess the differences in the relationship between species richness and both the number of trophic links and connectance across regions while controlling for sampling differences across studies. We assessed a total of 105 food webs across both tropical and temperate regions. We found that trophic taxa range from 10 to 142, number of links range 9 to 1880, and connectance varied from 0.0232 to 0.306. The models for both number of links and connectance predicted by species richness each had a significant interaction term (p = 0.000566 and p = 0.000642), providing strong evidence for regional differences in structural patterns. Our findings suggest that tropical food webs exhibit higher compartmentalization, likely due to greater trophic specialization, which reduces overall connectance. This reduced redundancy could make tropical food webs more sensitive to disturbances, potentially impacting their stability.
